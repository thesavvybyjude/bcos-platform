# Product Development & Market Entry

## Purpose
To provide frameworks for developing products in the CPG space, understanding market dynamics, and entering the market effectively.

## Core Philosophy

> "What is your vision? What do you think the world is going to be in 5-10 years and how does your product fit into that world?"

> "It's consistency, then you add intensity, and then you add technique. But people jump to technique because they feel like it's easier."

## Principles

### 1. Start with the Data
- Understand why the market is the way it is
- Don't just take numbers at face value
- Look at the trends beneath the surface

### 2. Stack Markets for TAM
- Find adjacent markets you can capture from
- Slate: Chocolate milk + Protein drinks + RTD coffee
- Each individual market creates a larger total addressable market

### 3. The "Better for You" Thesis
- Brands that win over the next decades are better for you
- The world is moving towards lower sugar, higher protein, cleaner ingredients
- "If you're just taking something that's bad for you and giving it a new face, you can buy the sales but the world is moving away from that"

### 4. Taste Always Wins
- Peter Rahal (RXBar): Taste always wins
- People won't sacrifice on taste for better ingredients
- You need both: great taste AND better ingredients

### 5. Kickstarter as a Launch Vehicle
- Build awareness, raise funds, create buzz
- Started with Kickstarter to fund first production run
- Whole Foods reached out because of Kickstarter

### 6. The Whiteboard Method
- Start with what you know (Google, LinkedIn)
- Meet one person, meet another person
- "We found this dude on LinkedIn and shot him a call"

### 7. The Retort vs. UHT Process
- Cans (retort): longer shelf life, brand identity
- Tetrapak (UHT): lower cost, different packaging
- Slate chose cans for brand identity and grab-and-go convenience

### 8. Ultra-Filtration Technology
- UF milk: sugar-free liquid protein
- Removes lactose while keeping protein
- "It literally tastes like cardboard, but it's so dense in nutrition"

### 9. The Google-to-Product Framework (Added by Manny Lubin)
- Step 1: Google it ("How to start a healthy chocolate milk company")
- Step 2: LinkedIn it (find an expert, cold call them)
- Step 3: Unlock #1 (learn about ultra-filtration)
- Step 4: Unlock #2 (shelf-stable process, choose the can)
- Step 5: Build (meet people, get in front of the right people)

### 10. The Retort vs. UHT Decision (Added by Manny Lubin)
- Retort (can/glass): longer shelf life, brand identity
- UHT (Tetrapak/plastic): lower cost, different packaging
- "The can is part of our brand, part of our identity"
- Energy drink market: people are used to grabbing and going
- Convenience piece was key

## Rules

- **Always start with the data** — Understand why the market is the way it is
- **Always stack markets** — Find adjacent markets to increase TAM
- **Always prioritize taste** — People won't sacrifice on taste
- **Always validate with real customers** — Focus groups, wear testing
- **Always get to market quickly** — Don't wait too long
- **Never compromise on product quality** — For a quick COGS improvement
- **Never ignore the "why" behind the numbers** — Skim declining, whole milk growing

## Heuristics

- If you can stack three markets, you've created a much larger TAM
- Taste wins over ingredients (even for founders who care about ingredients)
- The can is a brand identity, not just packaging
- UF milk tastes like cardboard, but it's pure protein

## Triggers

### When Developing a New Product:

```
New product idea

→ Ask: "What is the vision for the world in 5-10 years?"
→ Ask: "How does this product fit into that vision?"
→ Ask: "What markets can I stack?"

Then: Google it, LinkedIn it, find an expert.
"Meet one person, meet another person, meet another person."
```

### When Evaluating a Product Change:

```
Once you're established (5M+ in sales)

→ If you want to change the product:
    +-- Do proper research
    +-- Test with real customers
    +-- Don't make changes to save 2% on COGS

Example: Companies that changed their product to save money
and hurt their brand for years to come.
```

### Simon Squibb — Start with Passion, Not a Niche

- A business starts with a feeling, not an idea
- Follow your passions—don't listen to people who say that's not the way to start
- Your passions initially don't generate revenue because you haven't applied a business mindset to them
- "I love marketing, so I started a creative agency. There were 500 other businesses doing almost exactly the same. How did I win? I didn't have an original idea."

### Simon Squibb — The Love/Hate List

- Write down what you like doing and what you don't like doing
- Get really good at what you love
- Outsource what you don't like doing
- "The school system has taught you the wrong thing: if you're not good at something, you need to spend more time getting better at it. That is a lie."

### Simon Squibb — Combining Forces

- You don't need an original idea—you need to combine forces with other people
- Find someone who can execute on the thing you love but can't do yourself
- Example: Simon loved marketing, partnered with someone who could turn ideas into visuals—created a 50/50 business

### Simon Squibb — The Mind Map (vs. Business Plan)

- Business plans kill companies—they lock you in
- Mind maps keep you nimble and free
- Center: Your hobby/passion
- Branches: The Business, Network, Brands, Team, Partners, Merchandising
- Continue branching infinitely
- A mind map is infinite—a 20-page document is linear and restrictive

### Simon Squibb — Revenue Models

- Experiment with different ways to make money
- Don't limit yourself to one revenue model from the start
- Example: Instead of charging by the hour, charge by the outcome

### Simon Squibb — Purpose

- Make sure your purpose is strong
- If it's just about profit, everyone will want more money
- If you have a purpose bigger than yourself, you won't have to manage people—you only have to manage purpose

### Chris (VC) — The MVP + MVS Framework

```
MVP (Minimum Viable Product):
    │
    └── The smallest thing you can build that works
    └── "Viable" means you can build and deploy it
    └── Doesn't mean people will pay for it

MVS (Minimum Viable Segment):
    │
    └── The smallest customer segment you can dominate
    └── Where you can repeat the sale
    └── Proves your idea works

THE SEQUENCE:
    │
    1. Find your MVS (talk to 200 customers)
    2. Build your MVP for that segment
    3. Sell it to the MVS repeatedly
    4. Prove product-market fit
    5. Expand to adjacent segments
```

### Chris (VC) — The 200 Customer Rule

```
Before you build anything:
    │
    └── Talk to 200 potential customers
    └── Pattern recognition = find your MVS
    └── Ask: "What are your top 3 problems?"
    └── Ask: "How much would you pay to solve this?"
    └── Ask: "What do you use today?"

COST: Time only (no money spent)
RESULT: You'll know exactly who to target
```

### Chris (VC) — The Product-Company Gap

```
PRODUCT-MARKET FIT (The Start)
    │
    ├── You have a product people want
    ├── You've sold it a few times
    └── You have a class of customer
    │
    ▼
THE GAP ← THIS IS WHERE COMPANIES FAIL
    │
    ├── Can't scale sales
    ├── No repeatable process
    ├── Can't hire the right team
    └── No economic model
    │
    ▼
PRODUCT-COMPANY FIT (The Goal)
    │
    ├── Repeatable sales process
    ├── Scalable go-to-market
    ├── Economic model that works
    └── Company can run without you
```

### Alex Hormozi — The Grand Slam Offer Framework

```
THE VALUE EQUATION:
VALUE = (Dream Outcome × Likelihood) / (Time × Effort)

INCREASE THE TOP:
├── Bigger dream outcome
├── Higher likelihood of achievement
└── More proof and testimonials

DECREASE THE BOTTOM:
├── Faster time to result
├── Less effort required
└── Fewer obstacles

THE GRAND SLAM OFFER:
├── Dream Outcome: What does the customer want?
├── Problems: What prevents them from getting it?
├── Obstacles: What gets in the way?
├── Solutions: How do you solve each problem?
├── Mechanism: Your unique system
├── Guarantee: Reduce risk to zero
├── Bonuses: Incentivize speed
└── Sweetener: Make it irresistible

RESULT: 10x value for 10x price
```

### Alex Hormozi — The Selling Results Framework

- Sell a result, not a service
- "I'll help you lose 20 lbs in 8 weeks" > "I'll coach you for 8 weeks"
- People pay for outcomes, not inputs
- The more specific the result, the more they'll pay
- "If you're not embarrassed by your first offer, you launched too late"

### Alex Hormozi — The Offer Creation Framework

```
OFFER CREATION PROCESS:
├── Step 1: Identify the dream outcome
├── Step 2: List every problem that prevents it
├── Step 3: List every obstacle on the way
├── Step 4: Create a solution for each
├── Step 5: Bundle solutions into a mechanism
├── Step 6: Add a guarantee
├── Step 7: Add bonuses for speed
└── Step 8: Add a sweetener

THE GUARANTEE TYPES:
├── Unconditional: Full refund, no questions asked
├── Conditional: They do X, or you refund them
├── Anti-guarantee: "I won't give you a refund" (creates scarcity)
└── Partial: Refund a portion if they're not happy

THE BONUS TYPES:
├── Speed bonuses: Get results faster
├── Convenience bonuses: Less effort required
├── Access bonuses: Direct access to you
└── Service bonuses: Done-for-you elements
```

### WSJ — The Niche Focus Framework

```
ATHLETIC BREWING:
├── Exclusively non-alcoholic beer
├── 80 years of no innovation in the category
├── Athletic invented a new process
│
├── THE MOAT:
│   ├── $100M+ invested in manufacturing
│   ├── 2x water efficiency
│   ├── Dedicated non-alcoholic facility (450,000 barrels/year)
│   └── Hard for traditional breweries to enter
│
├── THE DATA ENGINE:
│   ├── 50%+ of early sales were DTC
│   ├── "Really fast feedback loops on what kind of beer they liked"
│   └── Data guided distribution and marketing strategy
│
└── THE RESULT:
    ├── Whole Foods top-selling beer across all beers
    ├── 1/3 of all category growth for 4 years
    └── 75,000 points of distribution

LESSON: Focus on a niche nobody is serving well
```

## Brian Tracy — The MVP & Customer Co-Creation

### The Lean Startup Approach

```
CUSTOMER CO-CREATION:
    │
    ├── Go to customers and ask: "How do you define a great product?"
    ├── "Would you buy this? Would you pay for it?"
    ├── "What do you want more of? What would you want less of?"
    └── "What can we do to improve it?"

THE MINIMUM VIABLE PRODUCT (MVP):
    │
    ├── Take a product idea to a customer BEFORE you even make the product
    ├── Work with the customer shoulder-to-shoulder to develop the product
    └── The customer says: "This is a great product" — THEN you produce and market it

THE RESULT:
    └── Customers become your preeminent salespeople
    └── They run around grabbing people and showing them the product
```
