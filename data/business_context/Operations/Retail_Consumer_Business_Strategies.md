# Retail & Consumer Business Strategies

## Purpose
To provide a comprehensive framework of proven business strategies from major retail and consumer brands—including Aldi, McDonald's, 7-Eleven, Sweetgreen, Shake Shack, Cava, Athletic Brewing, Liquid Death, and meal kit companies—that can be applied to businesses of any size.

## Core Philosophy

> "The success of the bottled water industry is really the success of the marketing industry."

> "Customers make compensatory inferences—when they see an item's price, they quickly make assumptions about the product."

## Principles

### 1. The Aldi Model (No-Frills Efficiency)
- Operational efficiency creates pricing power
- Smaller store footprint (12,000 sq ft vs. 31,000 sq ft average)
- Fewer products (1,600 vs. 31,000 at supermarkets)
- Limited staff (3-5 on floor vs. 10+)
- Products displayed in shipping boxes (less stocking time)
- The quarter cart system signals frugality (reduces labor costs)
- Private label dominance (90% of stock)
- Known Value Items (KVI) — staple products whose prices people remember

### 2. The Aldi Growth Cycle
- Thrives during recessions (2008 sparked 100 stores/year growth)
- Never lost customers when economy improved
- Inflation continues to drive growth

### 3. Meal Kit Economics
- 90% of new meal kit customers cancel within a year
- The discount trap: customers sign up for promotional rates, leave when prices normalize
- Economies of scale win (HelloFresh 78% market share vs. Blue Apron 6%)
- High-end differentiator: $17-$30/meal, no heavy discounts, profitable from year two

### 4. Sweetgreen (Premium Fast Casual)
- Positioned as a tech company, not just a restaurant
- Integrated AI/ML for personalization and loyalty
- Direct farm relationships (no distributors)
- Freshness is expensive but scales with automation
- Loyalty programs drive retention (Sweetpass: $10/month = $3 off one salad/day)

### 5. Shake Shack (Premium vs. Speed)
- Speed/quality tension: premium made-to-order vs. fast food speed
- Drive-through economics: McDonald's does 70% of US business via drive-through
- Efficiency through technology (start cooking when order placed)
- Licensed locations for rapid expansion (500+ locations, $4B company)

### 6. Cava (Acquisition as Growth)
- Bought Zoe's Kitchen (261 locations) for $300M
- Converting existing locations costs half of building new
- Data-driven site selection (demographic + psychographic + mobile analytics)
- Mediterranean is only 1.4% of US restaurants — growth category

### 7. 7-Eleven (Japanese Efficiency Model)
- Tanpin Kanri: localize assortment to customer needs
- Japanese model: multiple deliveries per day, data-driven ordering
- American model: 2 deliveries per week → 40% of products selling <1 unit/month
- Food is the growth category (tobacco declining, gas low-margin)
- Loyalty program: 95M members = massive targeting capability

### 8. Liquid Death (Brand Differentiation in Commodity Market)
- Bottled water is a commodity — brand is everything
- "Sell rebellion, not water"
- Organic marketing first (viral from the start)
- Distribution strategy: bars first, festivals, then retail
- Merchandise builds community (fans buy $30 t-shirts)

### 9. Athletic Brewing (Niche Focus + Manufacturing as Moat)
- Exclusively non-alcoholic beer — 80 years of no innovation in category
- $100M+ invested in manufacturing (hard to replicate)
- DTC as data engine (50%+ early sales through DTC)
- Whole Foods top-selling beer across all beers for over a year
- "More occasions" thesis — non-alcoholic beer is for more moments

### 10. McDonald's International (Localization)
- The 70/30 Rule: 70% global core, 30% localized menu
- India: No beef (McAloo Tikki), Canada: Poutine, Philippines: McSpaghetti
- Franchisee-led innovation (local teams know their markets)
- Successful local items can go global (McFlurry started in Canada)

## Decision Frameworks

### The Efficiency Signal Framework

```
When customers see a no-frills store:

SIGNAL 1: Cart deposit
→ "This store saves money on labor"
→ "The savings go to me"

SIGNAL 2: Products in boxes
→ "They don't waste money on fancy displays"
→ "The savings go to me"

SIGNAL 3: Limited selection
→ "They buy in bulk for lower prices"
→ "The savings go to me"

RESULT: Customer infers low price = efficiency, not low quality
```

### The Subscription Retention Framework

```
SIGN UP (Discount/Incentive)
    │
    ▼
SALES PITCH: "Just $4/meal!"
    │
    ▼
PRICE NORMALIZATION: "Actually $12/meal"
    │
    ├── VALUE JUSTIFIED → Stay
    │
    └── PRICE SHOCK → Cancel (90% do)

SOLUTIONS:
1. Gradual price increases (no dramatic changes)
2. Higher initial price + greater value = lower churn
3. Premium positioning = right customers
```

### The Speed/Quality Tradeoff Framework

```
PREMIUM POSITIONING
    │
    ├── Made to order, fresh ingredients
    ├── Customers want fast food speed
    │
    ▼
THE TENSION: How to speed up without sacrificing quality?

STRATEGIES:
1. Technology (start cooking when order placed, not when paid)
2. Process (pre-cut lettuce, time-motion studies)
3. Labor (send staff to bus orders)
4. Licensed locations (scale without full capital)
```

### The Acquisition vs. Organic Growth Framework

```
ORGANIC GROWTH:
    │
    ├── Find sites one by one
    ├── Build from scratch
    ├── Time: 18-24 months per location
    └── Cost: High (construction, real estate)

ACQUISITION:
    │
    ├── Buy existing chain
    ├── Convert to your brand
    ├── Time: 6-12 months per conversion
    └── Cost: Half of building new

CAVA'S MATH:
    └── $300M for 261 locations = ~$1.15M/location
    └── Half the cost of building from scratch
    └── 3x faster than organic expansion
```

### The Commodity Market Brand Framework

```
THE PROBLEM:
    │
    └── Product is a commodity
    └── Traditional branding: "pure, pristine, mountain spring"

THE SOLUTION:
    │
    ├── Sell rebellion, not water
    ├── Punk rock, skulls, counterculture
    └── "Marketing water like booze"
    │
    ▼
THE RESULT:
    │
    ├── Organic viral growth
    ├── Fans, not customers ($30 t-shirts)
    ├── Distribution in bars, festivals, retail
    └── $1.4B valuation

KEY INSIGHT: "The success of the bottled water industry is the success of the marketing industry."
```

### The Food Pivot Framework

```
HISTORIC REVENUE:
    │
    ├── Tobacco (profitable but declining)
    ├── Gas (low margin, at risk from EVs)
    └── Food/Snacks (stable demand)
    │
    ▼
THE PIVOT:
    │
    ├── Double down on food
    ├── Partner with Japanese supplier
    ├── New factories for localized food
    └── Data-driven inventory (95M loyalty members)
    │
    ▼
THE DATA ADVANTAGE:
    ── 95M loyalty members → Targeted ads → Impulse buys
```

### The Global/Local Tension Framework

```
GLOBAL BRAND:
    │
    ├── Core items (70% of sales)
    ├── Consistent quality and experience
    │
    ▼
LOCAL ADAPTATION:
    │
    ├── 30% of sales
    ├── "Tastes like home"
    ├── Local flavors and preferences
    │
    ▼
WIN-WIN:
    ── Global consistency + Local relevance
    ── New items can go global (McFlurry, McSpicy)
```

### The Niche Manufacturing Moat Framework

```
THE OPPORTUNITY:
    │
    └── Category stagnant for 80 years
    └── No one innovated
    │
    ▼
THE SOLUTION:
    │
    ├── Invest in manufacturing ($100M+)
    ├── New process (10-12 changes across stages)
    ├── 2x water efficiency
    │
    ▼
THE MOAT:
    │
    ├── Hard for traditional players to enter
    ├── Different food safety requirements
    ├── Different regulatory agencies
    └── Dedicated facilities
    │
    ▼
THE RESULT:
    ── Whole Foods best-selling beer across all beers
    ── 1/3 of all category growth for 4 years
```

### The Premium Service Economics Framework

```
PREMIUM POSITIONING
    │
    ├── Direct sourcing (higher quality, higher cost)
    ├── Fresh prep (labor intensive)
    ├── Tech investment (delivery, loyalty, AI)
    │
    ▼
CHALLENGE: Losses despite premium pricing
    │
    ▼
SOLUTIONS:
1. Scale (more stores = more volume = better economics)
2. Automation (Infinite Kitchen = 1/3 less labor)
3. Loyalty (Sweetpass = keep current customers coming)
4. Efficiency (pre-cut lettuce = less labor)
```

## Rules

### DO:
- Design your business to signal your value proposition
- Know your KVIs (Known Value Items)
- Private label can be a moat when you control the product
- Use data to drive inventory decisions
- Price staple items aggressively to signal low prices
- Invest in manufacturing when it creates differentiation
- Localize for your market (30% rule)
- Start with a niche nobody is serving well

### DON'T:
- Over-discount to acquire customers (price shock kills retention)
- Compete on price alone without operational efficiency
- Ignore category shifts (tobacco declining, gas at risk)
- Try to be everything to everyone
- Underestimate the power of brand in commodity markets

---

## Heuristics

- Operational choices communicate to customers (design them intentionally)
- Private label sales increased 34% industry-wide (2018-2022)
- 90% of meal kit customers cancel within a year
- HelloFresh takes 6 months to earn back $1 spent on acquisition
- Once you establish leadership, it's hard to disrupt
- Manufacturing can be a moat (hard to replicate)
- In commodity markets, brand is everything
- The quarter cart system signals to customers that cost savings are operational

## Triggers

### When Competing on Price:

```
COMPETING ON PRICE:
├── Don't just be cheap — be efficient
├── Signal efficiency, not low quality
├── Price KVIs aggressively (milk, eggs, bread)
├── Use operational choices as signals
└── The customer should infer: "savings go to me"
```

### When Scaling a Restaurant:

```
RESTAURANT SCALING:
├── Consider acquisition (Cava: 261 locations for $300M)
├── Drive-through economics are compelling (70% of McDonald's US sales)
├── Automation is the lever (Sweetgreen: 1/3 fewer staff)
├── Loyalty programs drive retention
└── Data-driven site selection reduces risk
```

### When Differentiating in a Commodity Market:

```
COMMODITY MARKET:
├── Brand is everything
├── Sell rebellion, not the product
├── Organic marketing first (viral from the start)
├── Distribution strategy: bars → festivals → retail
└── Merchandise builds community
```

### When Building a Niche:

```
NICHE OPPORTUNITY:
├── Find a category with no innovation
├── Invest in manufacturing as a moat
├── Use DTC data to guide distribution
├── Expand the use case (more occasions)
└── Focus on the one segment nobody is serving
```

### When Going International:

```
INTERNATIONAL STRATEGY:
├── 70% global core, 30% localized
├── Franchisees know their markets
├── Let local teams innovate
├── Successful local items can go global
└── "If it tastes like home and is true to the brand, it's a win"
```
