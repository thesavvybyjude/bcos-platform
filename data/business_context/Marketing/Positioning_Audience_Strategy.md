# Positioning & Audience Strategy

## Purpose
To provide a framework for positioning yourself as a creator to maximize brand value, attract the right audience, and command premium pricing.

## Core Philosophy

> "Every video that I'm going to put out from now on has to find its way into the group chats of people that I admire."

> "Positioning = Pricing Power."

## Principles

### 1. Positioning is Everything
- How you're perceived determines what you can charge
- Example: Alli Miller positioned as "#1 voice in AI business"
- Her views are lower but her audience is CMOs and decision-makers

### 2. Quality of Audience > Quantity
- 1M views from 16-year-olds = less value than 800 views from CMOs
- The right audience allocates capital

### 3. You Compete Against Bots
- 2023 competition: other creators
- 2025 competition: bots and machines
- News-style content is interchangeable and automatable

### 4. Your Brand is Your Moat
- Unique POV is irreplaceable
- AI can generate content but not your specific perspective

### 5. The Richer the Niche
- Hyperspecific is actually better
- Example: Nick Sarv (only AI automation content)
- $400,000/month from staying narrow

### 6. Position Yourself for Inbound
- Don't chase deals
- Create content that the right people find

### 7. Position to the Decision-Makers
- Every piece should be shareable in group chats of Fortune 500 CMOs

### 8. Vision Precedes Positioning
- You cannot position yourself effectively without a clear vision of where you're going
- Vision defines the destination; positioning defines how you appear on that journey
- "If you don't know where you're going, you can't lead others." — Mazi Sam Ohuabunwa

## Rules

- **Always position for the audience you want, not the one you have**
- **Always optimize for depth over reach**
- **Always stay hyper-niche**
- **Never sacrifice positioning for views**
- **Never create content that can't reach decision-makers**
- **Never let your brand be commoditized**

## Heuristics

- If a 16-year-old is your core audience, brand deals are low value
- If a CMO is your core audience, brand deals are high value
- 800 views from the right people > 1M views from the wrong people
- Your brand is the only thing AI can't replicate
- Stay narrow; broaden the business, not the niche

## Customer Avatar — Economic Grounding (Added by UI)

### Definition

A customer avatar is a detailed profile of your ideal customer. It must be based on **unit economics**, not feelings or assumptions.

### Key Questions

1. Who is your customer? (Demographics, psychographics)
2. What is their **budget**? (What can they afford?)
3. What is your **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)**?
4. What is your **Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)**?

### The Rule

The avatar must be **impersonal**. You are selecting a customer, not a life partner. Criteria should be objective and based on business economics.

### Example

- Fricki's avatar = university student who buys a Street Wise 3 at KFC (budget = R50-200)
- Why? Because his unit economics told him this customer profile makes the business viable
- If the avatar can't afford your product, the business doesn't work — no matter how much you like them

### Impersonal Criteria (Yes/No)

```
□ Can afford it?
□ Has the problem you solve?
□ Ready to buy now?
□ Can make the purchase decision?

Need 4/4 to qualify. No subjective criteria allowed.
```

## Identity, Emotion, Action Framework (Added by Colin & Samir)

### Three-Column Content Filter

```
When creating any piece of content, fill out:

| IDENTITIES | EMOTIONS | ACTIONS |
|------------|----------|---------|
| Who is this for? | What do I want them to feel? | What do I want them to do? |

This filters everything:
- Packaging
- Content
- Distribution
- Sponsorships

If you don't know who it's for, it's for no one.
If you don't know what they should feel, you can't connect.
If you don't know what they should do, you can't convert.
```

## TAM & Feasibility Analysis (Added by D Murphy)

### The TAM Trap Framework

```
THE STREETWEAR EXAMPLE:
+-- Started a men's streetwear brand
+-- If he went one inch left into women's, might have had a billion-dollar brand
+-- Men don't buy clothes like women do

THE LESSON:
+-- Clothing TAM is huge
+-- Men's streetwear at high price has a cap
+-- Many kids start streetwear brands—great marketing, design, production—but TAM isn't big enough

THE MATH:
+-- "I'm wearing a [__] 2-year-old shirt. I may never buy clothes again."
+-- Men are horrible customers (low consumption)

THE FEASIBILITY STUDY:
+-- What is the addressable market?
+-- Who are the customers?
+-- Is this a white space opportunity?
+-- What's your distribution?

THE RULE:
+-- People don't do this. They just start [__] all day.
+-- "I'm going to start a clothing brand."
+-- "Okay fine. In what category? In what market? What's your distribution?"
+-- No one thinks about that stuff.
```

### Simon Squibb — The 50% Rule

- 50% of what you spend on marketing will be wasted
- If you have a million views but the wrong views, it's wasted
- "Understanding who your customer is" is #1

### Simon Squibb — The Staircase Philosophy

- Do something wild and unexpected (like buying a staircase)
- News jack it—use it for PR and marketing
- Evolve it (add a doorbell for dreams)
- Get a partner to sponsor it
- "Marketing is meant to be fun"

### Simon Squibb — The Systems Approach

- Build a system for whatever marketing you do
- Better to do one platform well than many badly
- Example: One core video edited in-app for each channel

### Simon Squibb — Apply Your Marketing Love

- If you hate being on camera but love writing, do email marketing and LinkedIn
- Don't force yourself to do something that's not sustainable

## Value Proposition Validation (The 4 U's) (Added by iLab)

Before finalising your positioning, validate that you're addressing a valuable problem:

### 1. Unworkable
- Consequences are so great someone could get fired.
- Social problems: inequality, education gaps, health crises.
- **Question:** What happens if we don't solve this?

### 2. Unavoidable
- Taxes, death, aging, education.
- Things people must deal with.
- **Question:** Is this something people can't avoid?

### 3. Urgent
- Relative priority — you compete with time, money, and resources.
- Ask: "What's your number one priority right now?"
- **Question:** Is this a top priority for the user?

### 4. Underserved
- Not enough supply, lack of access, limited research/funding.
- **Question:** Are people not getting what they need?

### Minimum Viable Segment (MVS)
- Not the whole market — find one path with the same needs.
- Allows you to sell the same product repeatedly without changes.
- **Rule:** If you don't know who your customer is and think it's everybody, you'll fail.

## Latent vs Blatant Needs (Added by iLab)

| | Latent/Aspirational | Blatant/Critical |
|--|---------------------|------------------|
| **Type** | Nice to have | Must have |
| **Examples** | Entertainment, fashion | Food, water, health, job-critical |
| **Goal** | Move from latent to blatant | Stay here |
| **Question** | Is this nice to have or mission-critical? |

**Tip:** If your product is currently a nice-to-have, find the critical use case that makes it a must-have (e.g., iPad for pilots/doctors).

## Customer Discovery Questions (Added by iLab)

Don't pitch — ask everything:

1. "What's your number one priority right now?"
2. "What are the top 3 problems you're facing?"
3. "What happens if you don't solve this?"
4. "What are all the reasons you would not buy my product?"
5. "What would need to be true for you to adopt this?"

**Rule:** Ask the user to define the problem. They will tell you what's urgent, unworkable, and underserved.
