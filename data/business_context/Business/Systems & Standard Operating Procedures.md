# Systems & Standard Operating Procedures

The framework for building, documenting, and maintaining the operational systems that allow a business to scale beyond its founder.

## Purpose

This module covers the philosophy and methodology of business systemization — how to capture institutional knowledge into repeatable, trainable, improvable standard operating procedures (SOPs). The goal is to make the business run without its founder at the center of every decision.

## Principles

1. **Document What You Do, Then Improve It** — Don't wait for the "perfect" process before documenting. Capture the current process first, then iterate. An imperfect SOP is infinitely more valuable than an undocumented process.

2. **The 3-Layer SOP Structure** — Every SOP should have three layers: (1) a one-line purpose statement, (2) a bullet-point checklist for daily execution, and (3) a detailed reference guide for training. Most people only need layer 2.

3. **Owner, Not Author** — Every SOP must have a single owner responsible for its accuracy and currency. The owner is not necessarily the person who wrote it — it's the person accountable for the process outcome.

4. **If It Happens Twice, Systemize It** — The threshold for creating an SOP is low: if a task has been performed twice, it will be performed again. Document it on the second occurrence.

5. **Systems Enable Delegation** — You cannot delegate what you haven't documented. SOPs are the mechanism that turns dependent tasks into autonomous ones. Without systems, delegation is just wishful thinking.

6. **Version Control Everything** — SOPs must be versioned with dates and change logs. Team members should always know which version they're following and what changed.

## Rules

**Always:** Include a "last reviewed" date on every SOP. If it hasn't been reviewed in 90 days, it's potentially stale.

**Always:** Write SOPs at the reading level of the least experienced person who might use them.

**Never:** Create SOPs in isolation. The person performing the process must be involved in documenting it.

**Never:** Store SOPs in personal files or local drives. They must live in a shared, searchable, version-controlled system.

**Avoid:** SOPs longer than 2 pages for routine tasks. If it's longer, break it into sub-procedures.

**Prefer:** Video walkthroughs alongside written SOPs. Visual learners retain 65% more from video.

## Decision Frameworks

### SOP Priority Selector

If the process is high-frequency (daily/weekly) and involves multiple people:
- Then: Prioritize SOP creation immediately. This is a bottleneck risk.
- Otherwise: If the process is monthly or ad hoc → add to the SOP backlog for batch creation.

### SOP Retirement Decision

If an SOP hasn't been accessed in 6 months and the process it describes still exists:
- Then: Review and either update or archive. Do not delete — archive for reference.
- Otherwise: If the process no longer exists → archive the SOP with a note explaining why.
